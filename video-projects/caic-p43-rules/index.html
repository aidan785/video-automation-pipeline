The sorting insight
Three kinds of rules
Kind one
Recommendation
NIST AI Risk Management Framework
Voluntary guide from a US standards body. Nobody can fine you.
Shared vocabulary and a checklist to think with. Skip implementing all of it.
“It's absolutely not a checkbox.” The framework helps you ask how risky your system is.
Implement the parts that apply and you're already covering a lot of laws.
Kind two
Certification
ISO 42001
Audited and certified against it, like information security.
Costs money. Matters to enterprises that need the badge.
A two-location dental practice doesn't need it. Know it so you can say so.
Near miss: SOC 2 is general security assurance, not assurance about your AI system.
Kind three
Legislation
EU AI Act · US state laws
Sorts AI uses by risk. Bans a few outright.
Piles requirements on high-risk uses: employment and health decisions.
State laws keep changing: passed, repealed, replaced within two years.
High-risk decisions about people in a regulated place: a zone two conversation.
What an AI case looks like under rules that old
Overclaiming
“Our tool is 95% accurate,” with nothing behind it, is a deceptive claim
The fix is a habit: keep the internal write-up that shows the testing
The gas-leak flag: marketing that doesn't match how the product actually works
The case that shows the stakes
A pharmacy chain deploys facial recognition to catch shoplifters
Bought from a vendor. Never verified its accuracy. Pixelated photos. Untrained staff. It falsely accused customers, disproportionately women and people of color.
A 20-year enforcement order, opening with a 5-year total ban
You are the vendor in that story: sell honestly, back up your claims, test before deployment
